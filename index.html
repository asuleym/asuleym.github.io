About

Human–AI Interaction + Privacy + Language

I'm a research assistant at Kent State University, working with Dr. Lala Hajibayova. I study how people talk to AI, and what they leave out: privacy perception, self-censorship and trust in conversational AI, and the cultural side of how people understand privacy.

I trained as a translator and interpreter, and I speak Azerbaijani, English, Russian and Turkish. Before research I worked in data and product, and in 2020 I co-founded the EdTech startup Savadly. I build AI projects with Sundai at MIT, and I am applying to PhD programs in human-centered AI.

visual log

Photographs

People have always built tools to talk, write, remember and keep things private, and I see my research on how people talk to AI as the next step in that story. Behind each photo is one of those tools. Point at a photo, or tap it, to bring back its color and turn it over, then scroll the caption to read more.

Emoji ↗1999 · Shigetaka Kurita176 small pictures on a 12 by 12 pixel grid, made for NTT DOCOMO phones in Japan. MoMA added the set to its collection in 2016. A feeling you can send in any language, and a reminder that much of what we mean is never typed out.
Printing press ↗c. 1440 · Johannes GutenbergMovable metal type on a wooden screw press. Around 1455 Gutenberg printed about 180 copies of the Bible, and the written word began to travel without its author. Cheaper books opened learning to more people, the same idea that led me to co-found the EdTech startup Savadly.
ELIZA ↗1966 · Joseph WeizenbaumA short program that answered like a therapist. Weizenbaum later wrote that his secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private. It is still my question: what do people choose to tell a machine?
Rosetta Stone ↗196 BC · Memphis, EgyptThe same decree carved three times: in hieroglyphs, in Demotic and in Greek. Because one message survived in three scripts, Champollion could read hieroglyphs again in 1822. I began as a translator, and I still ask whether the language we use changes what we tell an AI.
Azerbaijani Latin alphabet ↗1991 · AzerbaijanAfter half a century of Cyrillic, Azerbaijan brought back the Latin script on 25 December 1991. The switch was complete on 1 August 2001, now the Day of the Azerbaijani Alphabet and Language. Its Ə still has no key on most keyboards: our tools are rarely built for every language equally.
Kodak box camera ↗1888 · George EastmanIt came loaded with film for 100 round photos, and you sent the whole camera back for printing. Two years later Warren and Brandeis wrote The Right to Privacy, warning that “instantaneous photographs” were reaching into private life. Every new tool raises that question again, and AI is the newest one.
Shakey the robot ↗1966 · SRIThe first mobile robot that could reason about its own actions. People gave it commands in plain English, and it planned a route, looked around with its camera and pushed blocks around its rooms. Today’s AI agents plan and act for us too, and part of my research asks what people are willing to let them do.
Dynabook ↗1972 · Alan KayAlan Kay described a notebook-sized computer “for children of all ages”: a machine for learning, drawing and writing, not only for calculating. Laptops and tablets still follow the idea. I came to research the same way, by building tools for learning.
Jacquard punched cards ↗1804 · Joseph Marie JacquardCards with punched holes told the loom which threads to lift, so a pattern could be stored and repeated. Ada Lovelace later wrote that Babbage’s engine “weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.”
Telegraph key ↗1844 · Samuel MorseLetters became short and long pulses on a wire. On 24 May 1844 the line from Washington to Baltimore carried its first message: “What hath God wrought.” The dots above spell its first word. Every new channel changes what people are willing to say on it.
Telephone ↗1876 · Alexander Graham BellBell’s telephone carried a voice down a wire in 1876. He answered calls with “Ahoy!”, but in 1877 Thomas Edison suggested “Hello,” and the word stuck, right down to the name tags.
Perceptron ↗1958 · Frank RosenblattA machine that learned from examples by adjusting the weight of each input. Rosenblatt’s Mark I learned to tell simple shapes apart. The networks behind today’s chatbots grew from the same idea.
Correspondence card ↗1869 · Emanuel HerrmannAustria-Hungary issued the first official postcard on 1 October 1869, for two kreuzer, less than half the price of a letter. Cheap and quick, and open for anyone along the way to read. People write differently when others might read, and that is where self-censorship begins.
Sholes and Glidden typewriter ↗1868 · Christopher Sholes & Carlos GliddenPatented in 1868 and sold by Remington from 1874. Its keyboard grew into the QWERTY layout most of us still type on, and delete on. Typing, rereading and deleting is often what self-censorship looks like, in a chat with AI too.
Mechanical Turk ↗1770 · Wolfgang von KempelenBuilt to impress Empress Maria Theresa, it seemed to play chess by itself and even played Benjamin Franklin in Paris. A chess master sat hidden inside the cabinet the whole time. It burned in Philadelphia in 1854, but its question remains: how much should we trust what a machine seems to do?
ARPANET ↗1969 · UCLA to SRIOn 29 October 1969 a student at UCLA tried to log in to a computer at SRI, 350 miles away. The system crashed after two letters, so the first message on the network that became the internet was “LO.” Each network since has carried more of our private lives.
Tinfoil phonograph ↗1877 · Thomas EdisonA needle pressed the vibrations of a voice into tinfoil on a turning drum. Edison recited “Mary had a little lamb” and heard it back: the first machine that could both record a voice and play it. Machines that remember are at the heart of my PhD plan: what should an AI keep from one conversation to the next?
Alberti cipher disk ↗c. 1467 · Leon Battista AlbertiTwo rings of letters, one turning inside the other. Turning the inner ring during a message changes the code as you write. Alberti described it around 1467, the first known polyalphabetic cipher. People have always wanted to share a message with one reader and keep it from everyone else.

Where people hold back

Four places where I study privacy and self-censorship: what people post, what they tell health tools, what they let AI agents do, and how culture shapes what privacy means.

Current study · Kent State University · Feb 2026 → now

Privacy perception and self-censorship in human–AI interaction

A qualitative study of 39 Eurasian-origin postgraduate and postdoctoral researchers based in the US, EU and UK, supervised by Dr. Lala Hajibayova. I lead participant recruitment, built the Qualtrics survey, run the semi-structured interviews, and transcribe, code and analyze the data in Python.
